Cost Analysis

Costing Approach and System Scope

This costing covers one Port Stewart unit, designed for replication across the other Lama Lama sites. All figures are in AUD and GST-inclusive where sourced locally. Two scenarios are presented: Scenario A places a LoRaWAN sensor at every site, while Scenario B substitutes standalone satellite monitors at the three remote sites if LoRaWAN coverage proves marginal.

The analysis follows five standard cost categories: material, labour, transport, training/implementation, and operating. Manufacturing labour is not shown separately because the system uses off-the-shelf Australian components, and a “discount for users” line does not apply because this is a grant-funded community project rather than a commercial product.

Capital Expenditure

Total CAPEX is approximately $16,182. Material costs account for about one third of the total, while the remaining costs reflect installation labour, freight to Cape York, Ranger training, community engagement, and contingency. This shows that the main cost driver is the remoteness of the site, not the design itself.

Operating Expenditure and Total Cost of Ownership

Annual OPEX is approximately $3,700. The largest component is Ranger inspection labour, shadow-priced at $2,000 so it remains visible in the total cost of ownership, even though it may be funded through existing Ranger programs.

Funding Pathways

The most direct funding option is the Indigenous Land Enterprise Infrastructure Fund (ILEIF), which offers grants up to $300,000 for remote infrastructure. A single ILEIF grant could fund roughly 18 Port Stewart units, leaving room for rollout to other Lama Lama sites.

Secondary funding options include the National Water Grid Fund’s First Nations stream and the Indigenous Land and Sea Corporation’s Our Country Our Future program. Ranger wages do not have to be drawn directly from this project budget.

Return on Investment

Non-revenue water typically accounts for 20–30% of supply in water utilities, and smart sensor networks can help recover substantial volumes. Port Stewart’s dry-season demand is around 25,000 L/day, based on a peak population of about 100.

A conservative 5% recovery would save approximately 456,000 L per year. Combined with avoided pipe-burst repairs and emergency water trucking, the annual benefits are expected to exceed the annual operating cost, giving capital payback within the first year on conservative assumptions.

Broader Considerations

Remoteness is built directly into the cost numbers. Cape York freight and remote contractor day-rates appear as their own line items rather than being hidden as general risks. Wet-season access restrictions are also considered, because remote monitoring allows leaks to be identified and prioritised even when physical access is limited.

The system also follows a “Maintenance First, Minimal Complexity” approach through modular components, a 10-year design life, and on-site spare parts. At the end of the system life, electronics and lithium-ion batteries can be returned to a metropolitan e-waste recycler through the same freight route used for delivery.
